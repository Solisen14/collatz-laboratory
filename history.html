BACKGROUND · THE HISTORY OF AN UNFINISHED QUESTION

Brief History of the 3n+1 Problem

One of the most famous unsolved problems in mathematics, the Collatz conjecture or 3n+1 problem, determines if every positive integer will iterate to 1 after performing repeated arithmetic operations. This problem has a simple procedure: an even iterate is halved; an odd iterate is multiplied by 3 and increased by 1, and the process repeats and generates a sequence. As an open, unproven problem, computer simulations indicate that this conjecture holds for positive integers up to 271 ≈ 2.36×1021. All of these values tested converge to 1, though a counterexample could still exist. Sequences can be depicted via corresponding graphs and conjecture tree visualizations.

From his study of graph theory and iterative functions in the 1930s, Lothar Collatz, from the University of Hamburg, developed these concepts and used directed graphs to represent these sequences. Despite not publishing his work, this information circulated throughout mathematics communities via oral presentations like the 1950 International Congress of Mathematicians in Cambridge, Massachusetts. Other names for this flourished such as the Syracuse Problem and the Hasse algorithm, while the 3n+1 problem was independently devised in 1952 and called the Ulam’s problem by Sir Bryan Thwaites. Another popular name given to this is the "Kakutani property" due to work in the 1960s by Japanese-American mathematician Shizuo Kakutani.

Despite its popularity, many such as Paul Erdős raised doubt that this could be proved using standard axioms of mathematics. In 2019 Fields Medalist Terrence Tao made the most modern advance attempting to show the orbits are bounded, while computers continue to verify the conjecture for increasingly massive positive integers. Recently, an AI-generated formal proof claimed to "disprove" the conjecture, but it was discovered to be a kernel bug.

The following quotation via John Lagarias captures the complexity of this problem: “The difficulty of settling the 3x+1 problem seems connected to the fact that it is a deterministic process that simulates ``random'' behavior. We face this dilemma: On the one hand, to the extent that the problem has structure, we can analyze it --- yet it is precisely this structure that seems to prevent us from proving that it behaves ``randomly.'' On the other hand, to the extent that the problem is structureless and ``random,'' we have nothing to analyze and consequently cannot rigorously prove anything.”

Jeffrey C. Lagarias, The 3x + 1 Problem and Its Generalizations (1985)

Defining the Collatz Function

This introductory mathematical explanation has been moved here temporarily while the History section is developed.

Begin with any positive integer. If the number is even, divide it by 2. If it is odd, multiply it by 3 and add 1. Because the rule depends on the input, we describe the process with a piecewise-defined function.

The notation C(n) means the result of applying the Collatz rule once to n. Every positive integer is either odd or even, so exactly one case applies.

For example, starting at 8 gives C(8) = 8 ÷ 2 = 4. Starting at 3 gives C(3) = 3(3) + 1 = 10.
