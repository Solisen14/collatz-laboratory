THE COLLATZ LABORATORY · BACKGROUND AND ORIGINS

History.

How did a simple arithmetic question become one of mathematics' most enduring mysteries?

First, remember the question

Defining the Collatz conjecture.

Begin with any positive integer. If the number is even, divide it by two. If it is odd, multiply it by three and add one. Repeat.

The conjecture is that every positive integer eventually reaches 1. No one has proved this for every positive starting value.

C(n)={ n2if n is even 3n+1if n is odd

Once a sequence reaches 1, the same rules continue.

1 → 4 → 2 → 1 → ⋯

Almost a century of curiosity

One question. Generations of mathematicians.

The problem spread from person to person, drawing new questions, new mathematical methods and new ways of seeing the same two rules.

1930sOrigins

Origins

An idea takes shape

In his student years, Lothar Collatz investigated graph theory and iterative functions. He became interested in drawing directed graphs of repeated arithmetic rules and asking what their paths might do.

Those ideas became central to the mathematical study of iterative sequences. The exact origin of the familiar 3n + 1 formulation is uncertain, but the question became associated with Collatz.

Meet the mathematician

Lothar Collatz

1910–1990 · Germany

A German mathematician whose research spanned numerical methods, graph theory and mathematical modelling. His interest in iteration helped give this enduring puzzle its name.

1950s–60sThe problem travels

The problem travels

One problem. Many names.

Collatz circulated his questions among mathematicians, including at the 1950 International Congress of Mathematicians in Cambridge, Massachusetts. Much of the problem's early history travelled by word of mouth.

The problem acquired many names: the Syracuse problem, Hasse's algorithm, Ulam's problem and Kakutani's problem. Bryan Thwaites independently encountered the problem in 1952. Each name records a different route through the mathematical community.

1978Other cycles

Other cycles

Beyond the positive integers

The original conjecture concerns positive integers. The same arithmetic rules also apply to zero and negative integers, where different repeating cycles appear.

0 → 0−1 ↔ −2−5 → −14 → … → −5−17 → … → −17

In 1978, Corrado Böhm and Giovanna Sontacchi studied the mathematics of cycles in generalized iterative systems. The known negative cycles open another line of investigation, but do not classify every negative orbit.

Difficulty and insight

Mathematics is not yet ready

Paul Erdős captured the extraordinary difficulty of the problem in a remark quoted by Jeffrey C. Lagarias in his influential 1985 survey.

This is a comment on the limitations of mathematical methods, not a proof that ordinary axioms cannot settle the problem.

Meet the mathematician

Paul Erdős

1913–1996 · Hungary

A prolific mathematician known for remarkable collaborations and deceptively difficult problems. He travelled widely to think and work alongside other mathematicians.

Lagarias described the puzzle as a deterministic process that simulates random behaviour. The fixed rule is straightforward; its long-term consequences remain stubbornly elusive.

1999–2004New landscapes

New landscapes

Into the complex plane

Researchers explored complex extensions of Collatz-like iteration. Work by Letherman, Schleicher and Wood, Dumont and Reiter, and Joseph L. Pe investigated how related rules can generate rich and sometimes fractal-like structures.

These are mathematical extensions, not a proof about the original positive-integer problem. And the familiar quadratic Julia and Mandelbrot sets illustrate a different iterative rule.

2015Seeing the sequences

Seeing the sequences

When mathematics becomes an image

As computers grew more capable, mathematical visualizations revealed the branching and shared portions of Collatz trajectories. A 2015 Wolfram Community post discussed Anton Bogatskiy's technique for drawing orbits with parity-dependent turns.

The resulting images can be astonishing. Sequences can be depicted via corresponding graphs and conjecture tree visualizations. But a visualization, however detailed, cannot establish the fate of every integer.

2019A modern breakthrough

A modern breakthrough

Almost all is not all.

Terence Tao showed that for almost all positive starting numbers, in the precise sense of logarithmic density, a Collatz orbit eventually reaches a value below any prescribed function tending to infinity.

This major result does not prove that every orbit reaches 1, or that the orbits remain bounded forever.

The search continues.

2025 · Computation

Immense verification, not proof

David Barina's published computation verified that the conjecture holds for all positive integers below 271, about 2.36 × 1021. All of these values tested converge to 1, though a counterexample could still exist. This is a milestone, not a proof of the infinite claim.

2026 · Proof checking

The AI-assisted false disproof

An AI-assisted purported disproof was accepted by a version of the Lean proof checker because of a software kernel bug, not because Collatz was disproved. The defect was fixed. The episode reminds us that even tools for checking proofs require careful scrutiny.
