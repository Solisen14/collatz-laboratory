04 / CAN WE PROVE ANYTHING?

Under 4n + 1, we can explain the endless growth.

Start at an odd positive integer, such as 27. Multiplying an odd number by 4 and adding 1 produces another odd number. And because 4n + 1 is greater than n, every step makes the value larger. It never becomes even, so it never gets divided by 2.

This isn't just what we observed on a finite graph. It proves that every positive starting value grows without bound under 4n + 1: even starting values are divided by 2 until they become odd, and from that point onward they increase forever.

How do we read this? Each curve represents one sampled starting integer. For a completed cycle, we draw backward from the smallest number in that cycle; for an unfinished calculation, from the last computed value. Parity controls turning, and the integer controls step length.

Are the number sequences converging? Not necessarily. The artistic overlay places every line at one pen position, even when the sequences head toward different cycles or hit our limits. Separated mode deliberately offsets those destinations to avoid a false impression of convergence; the canvas positions are not numerical coordinates.

Why can growing numbers make a tiny spiral? For exponent 1.2, large values produce drawing steps approximately proportional to r−0.2. Under 4n + 1, each successive odd value grows more than fourfold, so the pen movements shrink geometrically, even while the integers grow without bound.

What remains unknown? A cycle is recorded only if an integer actually repeats. Paths that stop at our 200-step or 1012 value limit are unfinished, not proved divergent. The picture offers patterns to investigate, not proof.

HOW TO READ THIS EXPERIMENT

Finite paths. Open questions.

Only positive starts. The directed graph uses starting numbers 1 to 25, positive a and b, and ordinary integer arithmetic; the Angle-Path Experiment can sample many more starting integers. All subsequent values are positive.

A shared directed graph. Every integer is one node, even when multiple starting values visit it. Each arrow points to the next value; cyclic arrows are shown explicitly.

Stopping rules. We continue through 1 and stop at the first repeated value, after 200 steps, or before exceeding 1,000,000,000,000. A repeated value closes a cycle; the outcome tells us whether that cycle contains 1. The map draws at most the first 32 edges per starting value, while the inspector reports the calculated path and its outcome. Reaching a boundary does not establish that a path tends to infinity.

No proof by picture. Changing a and b creates different dynamical systems. Seeing 25 paths does not establish what happens for all starting values.
