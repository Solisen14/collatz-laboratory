01 / THE BIG QUESTION

What are fractals, anyway?

Imagine zooming into a fern. Its smaller branches resemble the larger one. Zoom in again, and the same kind of shape seems to return. In mathematics, we can create objects whose detailed structure keeps revealing new structure, often with repeated or approximately repeated patterns at different scales.

Fractals involve simple rules that can be repeated endlessly.

A fractal is often built by iteration: apply a rule, take the result, and apply the rule again. The resulting image may show self-similarity, where smaller portions resemble the whole. Some fractals have exact self-similarity; others display much subtler detail.

Not every complicated picture is a fractal. Not every branching diagram is one, either. The important questions are how the object is defined and what happens to its structure as we examine it more closely. [1]

02 / A FAMILIAR SHAPE

Is the Collatz tree an infinite binary tree?

An infinite binary tree has endlessly many nodes, and each node can have up to two children. In a full infinite binary tree, every node has exactly two children.

In our Story, many starting values flowed toward the same positive cycle. When we reverse the arrows, a tree of predecessors grows outward. It certainly resembles an infinite binary tree. But look carefully at the branching.

THE IMPORTANT DIFFERENCE

Working backward from a number n, we can always find one predecessor by doubling it: 2n.

Sometimes we can find a second predecessor. Subtract 1 from n, then divide by 3. If the result is a positive odd whole number, it also leads to n under the Collatz rule.

For example, (10 − 1) ÷ 3 = 3, so 3 leads to 10. But (8 − 1) ÷ 3 is not a whole number, so 8 has no second predecessor.

When we draw the tree, we leave out the arrow from 1 back to 4, allowing us to show the repeating 1–4–2 cycle as a branching tree.

And there's a deeper question: does this backwards-growing component contain every positive integer? That's another way of asking the Collatz conjecture, not something our picture proves.

03 / BEYOND THE NUMBER LINE

One axis isn't enough anymore.

So far, Collatz has lived on a number line. But mathematicians can also work with complex numbers: numbers with a real component and an imaginary component. Every complex number has a position on a two-dimensional plane.

z = a + bi where i² = −1

The horizontal axis measures the real part, a. The vertical axis measures the imaginary part, b. For example, 2 + 3i lives two units to the right and three units up.

Whole numbers have not vanished. The number 3 is the point 3 + 0i on the real axis. We've simply given our mathematics another direction to explore. [2]

WHAT ARE THE COLOURS?

Each point in the picture follows the rule zn+1 = zn² + c. We repeat the rule and check whether the values move beyond a set boundary.

Black means the values have not escaped the boundary during the steps we calculated. They might still escape if we continue for longer.

Other colours show points that did escape. The colours tell us how quickly they crossed the boundary. Because the palette repeats, the same colour can represent more than one escape time.

In a Julia image we change the starting point z₀ and keep c fixed. In the Mandelbrot image we start at z₀ = 0 and change c. Switching colour palettes changes only how the picture looks, not the calculations.

Julia changes the starting point. Mandelbrot changes the parameter. The iteration is the same; the experiment is different.

05 / RETURNING TO COLLATZ

Can we bring Collatz into the complex plane?

Our original rule asks whether an integer is odd or even. But what would “odd” mean for 3 + 0.2i? It doesn't mean anything under the usual definition of parity. We need a new idea.

Extend the rule without losing the integers.

One approach is to construct an interpolating function: a function agreeing with our original Collatz rule at every integer, while also accepting real and complex inputs. This particular trigonometric interpolation is discussed in Marc Chamberland's survey alongside a related formula for the shortcut Collatz rule, where odd integers undergo an immediate division by 2. [5]

F(z) = (z/2) cos²(πz/2)+ (3z+1) sin²(πz/2)

At an even integer the cosine-squared term is 1 and the sine-squared term is 0, so the function halves the number. At an odd integer, the roles swap, giving 3n + 1. The original rule is preserved.

Beyond the integers, something completely new happens. We can iterate F(z) for complex z-values and investigate orbits, stable behaviour and the boundaries separating different outcomes.

One more twist: there's more than one way to extend Collatz.

The agreement on integers does not determine a unique complex function. Letherman, Schleicher and Wood investigated holomorphic extensions and their dynamics; Dumont and Reiter visualized other complex generalizations; Joseph L. Pe examined a different parity-inspired rule in the complex plane. [6–8]

Which extensions preserve the interesting questions about Collatz, and which lead us into an entirely new mathematical world?

06 / THE INVESTIGATION CONTINUES

The picture isn't the proof.

Fractals invite us to zoom in. Mathematics invites us to ask what the picture really means. The Collatz tree, Julia sets, Mandelbrot set and complex extensions of Collatz all begin with repeated rules. But the questions we can answer, and the ones we still cannot, are not the same.
